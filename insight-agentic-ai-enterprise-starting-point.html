Agentic AI in the enterprise: a practical starting point

TechSphire Insights · 8 October 2026 · 6 min read

AI agents can now plan and complete multi-step work across business systems. Here is how to pick the right first use cases and put the guardrails in place before you scale.

Generative AI assistants answer questions. AI agents go a step further: given a goal, they plan the steps, call the systems and tools they have been granted, check the results and hand back finished work. That shift — from producing text to taking action — is why agentic AI is moving quickly from experiments to operating budgets.

It is also why the first deployments deserve more care than a chatbot pilot. An agent that can update records, raise purchase orders or close tickets needs clear boundaries. The organisations getting value fastest are the ones that treat agents like a new kind of team member: scoped responsibilities, the right access, supervision and measurable outcomes.

Choose work that is frequent, rules-heavy and well documented

Good first candidates share a few traits. The process runs many times a week, follows documented rules with a manageable number of exceptions, and spans systems people currently copy information between. Typical examples include:

  • Service desk triage — classifying requests, gathering missing details and resolving routine issues such as access requests.
  • Invoice and order exceptions — investigating mismatches between purchase orders, receipts and invoices, and preparing the resolution for approval.
  • Customer onboarding — collecting documents, validating data across systems and keeping the customer informed.
  • IT operations — correlating alerts, running standard diagnostics and drafting incident summaries.

Avoid starting with processes that rely on tacit knowledge nobody has written down, or where a single error is costly and hard to reverse.

Design the guardrails before the agent

Most of the effort in a successful agent project goes into the environment around the model, not the model itself:

  • Least-privilege access. Give the agent its own identity with only the permissions its tasks require, and log every action it takes.
  • Human approval points. Start with the agent preparing actions for a person to approve, then remove approvals step by step as accuracy is proven.
  • Grounding in your data. Connect the agent to authoritative policies, knowledge articles and system records so it acts on facts rather than guesses.
  • Evaluation. Build a test set of real historical cases and measure the agent against it before go-live and after every change.

Measure outcomes, not activity

Agree the business measures up front: cycle time, cost per transaction, first-time-right rate, customer or employee satisfaction. Track them for the human process before launch so you have a baseline. An agent that completes many tasks but shifts rework to another team has not created value.

Plan for scale from the first use case

Once the first agent is working, demand arrives quickly. A small set of shared foundations prevents every team from rebuilding the basics: an approved platform and model catalogue, reusable connectors to core systems, a common approach to identity and logging, and a lightweight governance process to review new use cases for risk.

Where to begin

A focused readiness assessment — reviewing candidate processes, data quality, integration points and governance — typically identifies two or three use cases with a clear business case within a few weeks. From there, a time-boxed pilot with production-grade guardrails is the fastest way to prove value safely.
